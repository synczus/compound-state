**Bluntly: “Auto cycle — full squad sweep” is usually a lazy, overconfident plan that optimizes for *moving through space* instead of *surviving contact*.** It assumes the squad can simply sweep everything efficiently, but squad-level sweeps are exactly where people get pinned, split, ambushed, or forced into bad trades if they don’t isolate threats and manage angles. The core mistake is treating “sweep” as the mission rather than a method. [3][4]

What’s being overlooked:

- **Isolation of threats.** Good team fights are about forcing one enemy at a time, not peeking into multiple guns. If you expose the squad to overlapping fire, you hand the enemy a 3v1 or worse. [3]
- **Pinch risk.** The biggest danger is getting pinched from multiple angles; once that happens, squads die fast. A sweep that doesn’t control flanks is just walking into a crossfire. [3]
- **Marking and clearing the details.** In a real sweep, you don’t just “move through.” You mark hazards, search for wires, identify overwatch, and stay alert for booby traps and ambushes. [4]
- **Fatigue and time limits.** Even deliberate probing becomes unreliable when people are tired. The doctrine explicitly warns that probing is stressful and that time on task must be limited. [4]
- **The difference between hasty and deliberate.** A “full squad sweep” implies one-size-fits-all execution, but proper sweep doctrine distinguishes between quick visual inspection and more thorough methods, each with different risk profiles and requirements. [4]

What they should be doing instead:

- **Define the objective first.** Decide whether the goal is speed, certainty, survivability, or area denial. “Sweep everything” is not a strategy; it’s a workload. [4][5]
- **Break the area into sectors.** Assign responsibility so the team is not all looking at the same thing while a flank goes unchecked. [3][4]
- **Control angles and create isolation.** Move so that only one threat can engage at a time, and keep one element ready to give counter-pressure if contact develops. [3]
- **Treat hazards as a system, not a checklist.** Mark, search, confirm, and maintain security around any suspected danger before pushing forward. [4]
- **Use the right tempo.** If the situation is uncertain, slow down and make the sweep deliberate; if time matters, accept that a hasty sweep is incomplete by design. [4]

The real flaw is the hidden assumption that **more movement + more bodies = better clearance**. In practice, that often creates more exposure, more confusion, and more ways to get pinned. A better plan is **sectorized, controlled, and threat-isolating**, not a blind full-squad push. [3][4]
